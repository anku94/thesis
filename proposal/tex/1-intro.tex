The Bulk Synchronous Parallel (BSP) paradigm powers some of our most critical computational tools,
from large-scale scientific simulations to distributed machine learning.
These workloads---consisting of large parallel computations interspersed with I/O and global
synchronization---currently use upto O(10k)
nodes~\cite{Das2023:LargeScale,Stock2024:LargeScale,Gratt2024:Llama3Scale,:LlamaScale} and continue
to grow with time~\cite{Rahma2024:MLScale}.
As these applications scale, their efficiency has datacenter-level impacts on both cost and
time-to-discovery.

The tightly synchronized nature of BSP creates two unique challenges that strain modern computing
infrastructure.
On the data path, bursts of checkpointed state create massive I/O demands on the storage and
analytics infrastructure~\cite{Eisen2022:Checkpoint}.
On the compute path, global synchronization amplifies sensitivity to execution
variability~\cite{Hoefl2010:Noise,Petri2003:Missing,Yildi2016:IONoise}.
Performance bottlenecks that would be manageable in loosely coupled systems become critical scaling
barriers when every worker must wait for the slowest participant.

\section{Status Quo: A Nascent In-Situ Landscape}

In-situ analytics emerged as a promising response to these BSP challenges, attempting to leverage
idle computational resources during I/O phases to perform data analysis and organization tasks that
would otherwise require expensive post-processing.

Early in-situ work focused on tightly coupled libraries such as ParaView Catalyst, though these
were often limited to scripted visualization or basic filtering operations.
Loosely coupled approaches like ADIOS and DataSpaces enabled more sophisticated co-processing
workflows but introduced additional complexity and network dependencies.

Overlay networks including MRNet and LDMS provided scalable data reduction capabilities but offered
limited programmability, finding primary use in monitoring rather than general analytics.
In-situ indexing advanced with systems like DeltaFS implementing hash partitioning at scale, yet
adaptive range partitioning remained an unsolved challenge.

Despite these advances, most in-situ systems remained point solutions—valuable within their
specific domains but narrow in scope, with limited programmability or generality across different
use cases.

\section{Gaps and the Journey to Generality}

On the data path, the available toolkit for in-situ analytics remained fundamentally incomplete.
Most efforts focused on specific tasks such as visualization or compression, leaving open critical
challenges including adaptive range partitioning of streaming data.

On the compute path, the prevailing struggle centered on diagnosing and fixing performance issues.
While widely understood as fundamentally a load balancing problem, practitioners were plagued by
noisy telemetry and insufficient diagnostic tools to distinguish workload imbalance from
environmental factors.

This thesis initially approached these as two separate research problems.
Only through extensive empirical systems work did it become clear that both represented instances
of a deeper, more general challenge: organizing and processing streaming data effectively for both
insight generation and real-time control.

\section{Thesis Statement}

\textit{Streaming, programmable analytics and control can unify and solve both data and compute challenges in synchronized cluster computing.}

\section{Overview of Contributions}

This thesis presents three major contributions that progressively develop this unified approach:

\textbf{CARP} fills the last major gap in in-situ data organization by
providing adaptive range partitioning of streaming data, completing the toolkit of fundamental
partitioning primitives for in-situ analytics.

\textbf{CPLX/AMR} develops programmable, empirical workflows and tunable placement policies for real-world performance diagnosis and optimization, demonstrating the necessity of full-stack telemetry over traditional approaches.

\textbf{ORCA} generalizes these insights into a unified, real-time framework for observability and control at cluster scale, providing a programmable substrate for both data analytics and system optimization.
