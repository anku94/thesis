\chapter{Lessons from Optimizing Adaptive Mesh Codes}
\label{chap:amr}

\section{Motivation: Computational Efficiency in AMR Codes}

Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR) is a widely used technique for efficiently simulating physical
systems that exhibit large variations in spatial or temporal scales, such as astrophysical
phenomena, fluid dynamics, and climate modeling.
AMR codes dynamically refine and coarsen the computational grid to concentrate resources in regions
of interest, resulting in highly irregular and rapidly evolving mesh structures.
This approach enables fine-grained resolution where needed, but creates significant computational
challenges: the workload becomes highly imbalanced, dependencies between tasks grow complex, and
the dynamic nature of the mesh makes it difficult to achieve efficient parallel execution and
balanced load across compute resources.

Our goal was to develop placement policies that leveraged runtime telemetry to improve placement
and efficiency.
Access to our research cluster enabled us to experiment with fine-grained telemetry and placement
under full-stack administrative control, providing the ability to analyze and intervene at all
levels of the software and hardware stack.

\section{Challenges of Telemetry-Driven Placement}

Developing telemetry-driven placement policies in AMR codes exposed several
key challenges:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Establishing measurement fidelity.}
        Initial telemetry was often unreliable and did not match intuition.
        Only by using full-stack access and low-level debugging could we identify and correct sources of
        error across hardware, drivers, MPI, and the application.

  \item \textbf{Scaling analysis for high-volume telemetry.}
        Analyzing per-rank, per-timestep telemetry required moving beyond standard profiling and tracing
        tools to query-driven workflows that could surface root causes from massive data.

  \item \textbf{Low-overhead, effective placement.}
        Even with reliable telemetry, incorporating it into placement imposed strict timing constraints and
        required new algorithms to balance objectives like load and locality in dynamic, NP-hard settings.
\end{itemize}

\section{Contribution 1: A Diagnostic Workflow}

The process of developing placement policies required understanding what our telemetry was
capturing.
Initially, our telemetry showed no correlation between expected workload drivers and observed
performance (Figure~\ref{fig:amr-tele-reg}).
Deeper investigation revealed cross-stack artifacts: fail-slow hardware, system tuning issues, and
fabric-level problems that had to be systematically mitigated.
Only after this full-stack debugging and tuning process could we obtain reliable telemetry that
correlated with expected application behavior.

While our full-stack access enabled these investigations, we found identifying and isolating these
transient challenges to be difficult.
Our workflow started with standard profiling and tracing tools.
The former did not capture these artifacts, and the latter provided too much unstructured detail.
We organically evolved a workflow that allowed targeted collection of structured and queryable
telemetry, starting from CSV exports and pandas analysis, and culminating in ClickHouse and SQL
queries.
This custom approach enabled correlation of performance data across ranks and system layers,
allowing us to isolate the true sources of performance deviation.

\section{Contribution 2: The CPLX Placement Policy}

With reliable telemetry established, we turned to the challenge of developing placement policies.
Placement conventionally considers locality maximization to be a fundamental tenet, with
Space-Filling Curves co-locating spatially adjacent grid blocks to preserve communication locality.
However, we found locality maximization to be at odds with computational load balance.
After substantial experimentation, we identified this as the core tradeoff requiring explicit
management rather than assuming one objective should always dominate.

CPLX provides predictable control over the tradeoff between locality and load balance through a
single tunable parameter X.
At X=0, CPLX is locality maximizing; at X=100, CPLX is pure computational load balance.
CPLX is a hybrid of two policies: CDP (a dynamic programming-based locality maximizing policy) and
LPT (Longest Processing Time).
The algorithm computes an initial placement using CDP, then examines allocated load among ranks and
only rebalances X\% of the most overloaded and underloaded ranks, preserving locality for
well-balanced portions.

\section{Key Results}

Our evaluation was conducted on an in-house research cluster with full-stack administrative
control.
Experiments scaled from 512 to 4096 ranks to evaluate performance across different system sizes.
We used the Sedov blast wave simulation, considered a standard challenging AMR problem with dynamic
refinement patterns.
Baseline comparisons were made against already-optimized placement policies including standard
Space-Filling Curve approaches, with fine-grained telemetry collection enabling precise measurement
of placement policy effects.

CPLX achieves up to 21.6\% runtime reduction over already-optimized baselines.
Performance follows a U-shaped curve with best performance at intermediate X values
(Figure~\ref{fig:amr-eval-rt}).
We observe diminishing gains in synchronization time after moderate X values, reflecting
diminishing computational load balance gains.
Performance degradation at high X values occurs due to locality penalties offsetting load balance
benefits.
Gains from load balancing increase with scale, demonstrating that larger systems benefit more from
the hybrid approach.
These results show that neither pure locality (X=0) nor pure load balance (X=100) is consistently
optimal.

\section{Lessons}

Elevated synchronization time is traditionally ascribed to algorithmic load imbalance in
performance analyses, but while system variability issues are known, they are not systematically
factored into diagnosis.
Our study demonstrates the risks of this approach---many "load imbalance" problems are actually
cross-stack variability masquerading as algorithmic issues.
The biggest bottleneck was understanding performance rather than algorithmic optimization, as
performance characteristics are inherently idiosyncratic to specific hardware and software stacks.
This highlights the need for real-time visibility into application performance for effective
diagnosis and tuning.
Our structured telemetry pipeline demonstrates the path forward from ad-hoc tools to queryable,
programmable observability that can be made real-time, enabling adaptive control loops and
reframing performance tuning as distributed state reconstruction requiring streaming analytics
capabilities.
